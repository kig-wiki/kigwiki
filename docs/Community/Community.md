# Kigurumi Community

There is no single “Kigurumi community.” The hobby includes individual performers, friend groups, online spaces, convention meetups, and private photoshoots. People of many genders take part for cosplay, performance, craft, identity, creativity, or other personal reasons.

Buying a Kigurumi Mask does not guarantee access to a group, but shared interests can create opportunities to meet people. Approach those connections as relationships, not as benefits included with a purchase.

## Consent and boundaries

A costume never implies consent. Ask before touching, hugging, and so on. In a group or photoshoot, it helps to discuss boundaries before masks go on, such as:

- What kinds of contact are welcome?
- May photos and videos be posted, tagged, or shared privately?
- What identity does the performer want to referred by? Some might want to just "be in character" and be referred to as such, worth asking rather than assuming.
- Any parts of a costume should only the owner handle? Or any concern areas to keep an eye on to help them look their best?

Consent can be changed or withdrawn at any time. If someone becomes uncomfortable, stop. A performer can and should speak up. Break character, leave, or unmask whenever needed for safety or comfort, dont live in absolutes.

Some adults also participate in kink or create adult content, much as we do in real life, with cosplay, in furry scenes, and so on. That does not define Kigurumi Mask cosplay or imply anything about another performer, consent is even more critical in these situations.

## Communicating while masked

Kigurumi Mask performers may be quiet or nonverbal while in character, but clear communication still matters. Agree on simple gestures, phone messages, or help from a [handler](../Start/handlers-guide.md). Do not make someone stay silent when they need to address safety, consent, heat, visibility, or navigation.

Because facial expressions are fixed and body language can be harder to read, avoid relying on hints. Ask direct, respectful questions and accept the answer without pressure.

## Meeting local kigs

Conventions, public social accounts, and introductions through mutual friends are common ways to find local kigs. Private gatherings may not be publicly advertised, and an invitation to one event is not an open invitation to every group.

A small card with a character photo and public social account can make exchanging details at a convention far easier.
Casual meetups without costumes are also pretty common ice breakers when first meeting someone new, a "vibe check" if you will. They can also help people discuss expectations before planning a masked outing or photoshoot.

## Contributing to a group

Good group experiences come from communication and practical cooperation. Offer help that fits the situation: watch belongings, take a turn with photos, check costume details, share scheduling information, or help someone navigate. Do not assume every participant has the same time, money, equipment, confidence, or performance style.

Judge behavior directly. Mask price, maker, appearance, and experience level do not establish whether someone is trustworthy or considerate. Respecting boundaries, reliability, honesty, and good behavior does.

## Online spaces

Open social spaces can expose you to only one slice of the hobby, experienced performers and makers honestly dont tend to spend much time in those spaces, if at all. For better or worse, many of those spaces tend to have many more kigfans than kig performers or kig makers. Hot takes by those without much experience are a dime a dozen.

A bad interaction does not represent every performer, and sharing a hobby does not require anyone to become friends.
Many kigs get a lot of "how do I start" messages, if you feel ghosted, don't take it personally. Repeating those answers regularly is one reason this wiki exists, information discovery is still a weak spot of the hobby.
